I have never been so conflicted about a book. Because Babel has one of the coolest premises I've ever read. Magic through translation? Silver bars that lose meaning across languages? An alternate Oxford where language is literally power and the British Empire built its dominance on it? I was sold before I even opened page one.

And honestly? The idea itself is brilliant. The discussions around language, translation, the things that can never quite be translated from one culture into another — those were easily my favourite parts. You can tell R.F. Kuang loves languages, and that passion comes through in every footnote, every linguistic tangent, every explanation of how meaning shifts between cultures.

Unfortunately … I don't think the rest of the novel quite lives up to that premise. My biggest issue wasn't even that the book is “heavy-handed.” I don't mind books that are unapologetically political. Colonialism was horrific. It doesn't need a both-sides argument to justify criticising it.

My problem was that the novel rarely trusted the reader. Instead of letting the story naturally arrive at its themes, it often felt like the story paused so the author could explain them to me. I wasn't always reading Robin's thoughts — I often felt like I was reading Kuang's. The characters sometimes sounded less like students living in 1830s Oxford and more like modern university students dropped into period clothing. It constantly reminded me I was reading a book instead of living inside its world.

I don't think a single character truly surprised me. Robin is sympathetic, Ramy is exactly who he appears to be, Victoire deserved more page time, and Letty — I know she's one of the most divisive characters in the book, but I wish she'd been written with more complexity. Not because I wanted redemption for her, but because I wanted unpredictability. I kept waiting for the story to challenge my expectations of her, and it never really did.

The fantasy itself also left me wanting more. The silver-working system is fascinating on paper, but I never felt like the novel fully explored its possibilities beyond serving as a metaphor. It sometimes felt less like a magic system and more like a vehicle for the book's politics — which is fine, except when your world is this interesting, I naturally wanted it to stand on its own too.

That said — I understand why so many people adore this book. If you've ever felt caught between cultures, between languages, between belonging somewhere and being treated like an outsider despite giving everything to that place, Babel probably hits incredibly hard. There are moments that feel painfully real despite the fantasy setting.

> It had so much to say… that it rarely stopped long enough to let the story say it for itself.

I just found myself wishing the novel trusted its world — and its readers — a little more. Fewer lectures. More ambiguity. More room to disagree. More room to think.

I admired this book more than I loved it. And somehow, I think that's the most Babel thing I can say.

```verse
Its words were clever, sharp, and bright,
They filled the page with borrowed light.
I loved the dream, not all the way,
But still, its echoes chose to stay.
```
