I have absolutely no fucking clue how to rate this book. Did I enjoy reading it? Not really. Do I think it's brilliant? Unfortunately, yes.

This book frustrated me for almost its entire runtime. Nothing seems to happen. Then everything happens. Then somehow … nothing has still happened. Josef K. is arrested in the first sentence for a crime nobody will tell him about. He spends the rest of the novel trying to understand what he's even accused of, meeting people who supposedly know how the system works, only to leave every conversation knowing even less than before.

At first, I kept waiting for the plot to start. It never really does. And that's when it hit me. That's the plot. Kafka isn't interested in solving the mystery. He's interested in watching a man slowly exhaust himself trying to force logic onto something that has none.

Honestly? That's life. We spend so much of it trying to understand why shit happens. Why this person died. Why that relationship ended. Why good people get fucked over while awful people keep winning. We invent reasons because our brains need reasons. Kafka just looks you dead in the eye and says, “What if there isn't one?”

> What if there isn't one? Rude. But fair.

The entire novel feels like a fever dream where everyone except you has already read the rulebook. Characters appear. Give cryptic advice. Disappear. Nobody reacts to anything like a normal human being. Everyone treats the absurd as if it's Tuesday afternoon. And somehow … you start doing it too. By the end, I wasn't even questioning the insanity anymore. I just accepted it. Which is honestly even more terrifying.

The “Before the Law” parable completely derailed my brain. I had to stop reading afterwards because Kafka and the priest proceed to analyse it from multiple angles, and somehow every interpretation makes sense while also making absolutely no sense. Was the man deceived? Did he deceive himself? Was the guard lying? Does it even matter? I don't know. And I don't think Kafka gives enough of a shit to tell me.

The ending genuinely pissed me off. Not because it was bad. Because it felt inevitable. Josef K.'s final words — “like a dog.” Fuck. That line landed harder than I expected. Not because of the execution itself, but because by then he'd been stripped of everything that made him a person. His dignity. His certainty. His agency. Even his innocence stopped mattering, because innocence means fuck all inside a system that never intended to judge you fairly in the first place. Whether that system is bureaucracy. Religion. Society. Life itself. Take your pick.

I also love how Kafka refuses to explain a goddamn thing. Where are we? No idea. What law? Who made it? Why Josef? What crime? Good fucking luck. Normally that would drive me insane. Here … it somehow becomes the point. The less Kafka explains, the more universal the story becomes. We've all been Josef K. at some point. Stuck in systems we don't understand, following rules nobody can explain, apologising for things we don't even know we did wrong.

Reading this book felt exactly like standing in line at a government office where every employee keeps sending you to a different counter. Kafka really invented bureaucracy as psychological horror.

I don't think I understood half of what Kafka was trying to say. Actually, I'm pretty sure I didn't. But every now and then he'd drop a paragraph or a metaphor so brilliant that I'd have to put the book down and just … stare at the ceiling for a bit. That's an annoying level of genius.

I hated reading this. I can't stop thinking about it. Which is probably the highest compliment I can give.
