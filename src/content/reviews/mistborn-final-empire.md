I went into Mistborn expecting a fantasy heist with a cool magic system, and somehow came out emotionally attached to a bunch of criminals trying to overthrow an immortal tyrant.

So. That happened.

The premise itself is ridiculously good: a world where the evil won, the chosen one failed, and the Lord Ruler has been sitting on the throne for a thousand years while the skaa live under brutal oppression. And instead of giving us another story about someone discovering that they are destined to save the world, Sanderson starts with a much more interesting question:

> What if you just decided to overthrow the guy who already won?

That's where Kelsier comes in, with his absolutely unhinged confidence and a plan that sounds increasingly insane the more you think about it. He wants to do what everyone else has apparently decided is impossible—bring down the Lord Ruler and destroy the Final Empire.

And naturally, he assembles a crew.

This was one of my favourite parts of the story because the crew actually feels like a crew. Everyone has a particular skill, personality and role, and you slowly understand why Kelsier chose them. They're not just there to stand around while the main characters do everything. They're thieves, spies, fighters, informants and specialists, and watching the plan come together through all of them made the whole rebellion feel much bigger.

And then there is Vin.

Vin starts out as someone who has basically learned to survive by trusting absolutely no one. She isn't some instantly confident badass who walks into the story knowing exactly who she is. She's suspicious, frightened, angry and very much lost.

And somehow, the rebellion becomes part of her journey too.

While Kelsier is teaching her Allomancy and pulling her into his world, Vin is learning what it means to actually belong somewhere. The story isn't just about whether they can overthrow the Lord Ruler anymore. It's also about whether Vin can stop seeing herself as someone who only survives and start seeing herself as someone who can actually choose what she becomes.

That part really worked for me.

Allomancy took me a while, though. At first I was genuinely like, okay, cool, people swallow metal and apparently this is going to make sense eventually.

And eventually, it did.

Once the system clicked, I loved how Sanderson used it in the actual story rather than treating it as a list of cool powers. The abilities become part of the heists, fights, training, escapes and strategy. Suddenly you're not just reading about what the characters can do; you're trying to figure out what they might do with it.

The worldbuilding is similarly detailed. The Final Empire feels oppressive in a way that's difficult to ignore—the distinction between nobles and skaa, the way society functions, the mythology around the Lord Ruler, the religion and history behind everything. You can actually picture the world rather than just being told that it's dark and miserable because the author said so.

I did struggle with the middle.

There were definitely sections where the story slowed down and I was waiting for things to move again. The politics and planning sometimes felt like they were taking the scenic route to wherever we were eventually going.

But then Sanderson apparently decided that patience deserved compensation.

Because that ending.

The final stretch is basically the book taking everything it has been building toward and setting it on fire.

The twists genuinely twisted.

What I liked most is that the reveals don't feel like Sanderson pulling random answers out of a hat. The foreshadowing is actually there. You just don't necessarily recognise what you're looking at until the book finally puts the pieces together for you.

And the action sequences? Bloody hell.

The combination of Allomancy, movement, strategy and sheer chaos makes some of the fights ridiculously easy to visualise. It felt cinematic without needing the prose to become unnecessarily dramatic.

I also really liked that Sanderson doesn't rely on romance to carry the emotional weight of the story. I understood Vin and Elend, but I didn't really feel their relationship. For me, the stronger emotional connections were elsewhere—in Vin's relationship with Kelsier, the crew, and the strange little sense of belonging she gradually develops.

Sazed deserves his own paragraph simply because Sazed is lovely.

And Spook is cute. I don't make the rules.

Sanderson's writing is straightforward and sometimes over-explains things, but honestly, with a world this complicated, I didn't mind it nearly as much as I thought I would. Once I understood what was happening, the simplicity actually made the book very easy to get lost in.

And now I understand why people told me to trust the process.

Because Mistborn spends a lot of time making you learn its world, its rules and its people—and then the ending makes you realise why it bothered telling you all of that.

> I don't rate books in stars. I think I prefer measuring them by the intensity of whatever emotion they leave behind.

Mistborn left behind excitement, curiosity, affection for fictional criminals, a newfound trust in metal-based magic systems… and a very strong need to know what happens next.

So yes.

I loved it.
