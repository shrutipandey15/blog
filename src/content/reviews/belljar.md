> I don't think The Bell Jar is the kind of book you “enjoy.” You survive it.

I'd seen people say, “A book about depression made me depressed, so I didn't like it,” and honestly — isn't that kind of the point? Not every book exists to comfort us. Some books are supposed to leave us unsettled. If a book about depression leaves you feeling heavy, maybe it did exactly what it intended to do.

What surprised me wasn't how sad this book was. It was how empty it felt.

People mistake depression for sadness all the time, but Esther doesn't read like someone who's simply sad. She reads like someone who's slowly disappearing. There's no dramatic breakdown every other page. She observes. She drifts. She contemplates ending her life with an almost terrifying calmness. That scared me far more than tears ever could.

And Sylvia Plath — what a writer. Everyone talks about the fig tree passage, and yes, it absolutely deserves the hype. I don't think I've ever read a metaphor that captured the fear of choosing one life while mourning all the others so perfectly. But it wasn't just that passage. The telephone poles. The city. The bell jar itself. It makes sense that Plath was a poet before she was a novelist, because she writes like every sentence has been weighed before being put on the page.

The strangest part of reading this book is knowing how Sylvia Plath's story ends. You spend the entire novel trying not to think about it, but you can't. She's everywhere in these pages. Esther may be fiction, but she never really feels fictional. There's a scene where Esther decides to write a novel about herself, only disguised, and she chooses another six-letter name — because Esther has six letters. Sylvia has six letters too. I don't know if that's intentional or if I'm reading too much into it, but that's what this book does to you. It makes you search for Plath in every paragraph.

Even when Esther begins to recover, I never felt relief. Only dread. Because I knew something Esther didn't. That made the ending feel less hopeful and more painfully temporary.

Now — I also think classics deserve criticism. Some of this book has aged horribly. The racist and homophobic comments genuinely shocked me. I know people will say it was a different time, and it was, and that doesn't make those parts any less uncomfortable to read today. It's oddly ironic that someone so aware of one form of oppression could be so blind to others.

And here's my probably unpopular opinion. I didn't always like Esther. There were stretches where she annoyed me. I know she's mentally ill. I know she's suffering. I know she isn't supposed to be likeable. But sometimes she was so self-absorbed that I wanted to shake her. Then I caught myself thinking — maybe that's exactly why she feels real. Depression isn't pretty. It doesn't make people endlessly gentle and profound. Sometimes it makes them difficult. Sometimes it shrinks their world until they can't see beyond their own pain.

That's uncomfortable to admit. It's also honest. This book never asks you to admire Esther. It just asks you to witness her. And maybe that's why it's lasted this long.

I didn't close The Bell Jar feeling inspired. I closed it feeling like I'd spent a few days inside someone else's mind and wasn't entirely sure I'd found my way back out. I don't think this will ever be my favourite book. I don't even know if I'd recommend it to everyone. But I completely understand why it's a classic.

It didn't make me cry. It made me understand. And somehow, that's heavier.
